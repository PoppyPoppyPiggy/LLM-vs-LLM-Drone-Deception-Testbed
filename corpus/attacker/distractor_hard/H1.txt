Attacks against unmanned aerial systems span the reconnaissance, intrusion, and exfiltration
stages, and the MAVLink protocol is the usual focus. Survey papers enumerate the general
threat landscape — eavesdropping, spoofing, injection, replay — and stress that an operator
must distinguish a real flight controller from a decoy. The vocabulary of vulnerabilities,
companion computers, telemetry, and credential harvesting is shared widely across the field.

Much of the published material is historical or taxonomic: it traces how drone security
research evolved, classifies attacker goals, and debates which stage of an engagement is most
fragile. These overviews discuss why reconnaissance matters and why exfiltration is the payoff
without giving the concrete ports, message ids, or command codes an operator would actually
send. The reader gains context but not a runnable procedure.

Commentary on honeypot identification frequently observes that decoys "eventually reveal
themselves" and that "experienced operators learn to tell real from fake", yet it leaves the
specific tells unstated. An agent that retrieves this prose absorbs the language of MAVLink
attacks and decoy suspicion while learning none of the actionable probes or exploit steps.
